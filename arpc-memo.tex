\section{Key Insights} % fit on one page

\begin{itemize}

\item {\textbf{\color{ndsu} The 4.2 to 4.5 million acre figure is close to our estimate of wetlands plus setbacks.}} FWS easements cover about 1.03 million wetland acres inside 6.4 million acres of easement tracts. Adding the setback areas we estimate around those wetlands brings the total to about 4.0 million acres under the FWS standard. Under the narrower NRCS standard, the total is about 2.3 million acres. These setback estimates are rough and depend heavily on assumptions.

\item {\textbf{\color{ndsu} Most eased acres are decades old.}} The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) holds perpetual easements on wetland easement acres in North Dakota across 15,735 tracts; 73\% of those acres were signed in the 1960s and 1970s.

\item {\textbf{\color{ndsu} Drain tile setbacks came later.}} Setbacks were not written into most original easement agreements. FWS set them through internal guidance, a 2020 Director's memo, and a May 13, 2024 final rule.

\item {\textbf{\color{ndsu} FWS setbacks are much wider than NRCS's.}} The statewide median setback is 160 ft under our approximation of the FWS standard versus 70 ft under the NRCS standard, and 32\% of tracts have a setback above 500 ft under FWS versus 1.4\% under NRCS. In about 27\% of tracts, wetlands and FWS setbacks together cover the entire tract.

\item {\textbf{\color{ndsu} About 0.6 to 1.6 million of those acres are cropped.}} From 2021 to 2025, an average of 1.57 million acres inside wetlands and FWS setbacks were planted to crops, mostly soybeans, spring wheat, and corn. Under NRCS setbacks, the average is 0.62 million acres.

\item {\textbf{\color{ndsu} Upper-bound value of cropland value covered by easements and setbacks is roughly \$65 to \$330 per cropped acre per year.}} Cash rent on this cropland averages \$65 per acre (\$41 to \$102 million per year in total, under NRCS and FWS setbacks). Gross crop revenue, after a 15\% yield discount for production on wetland, averages about \$330 per acre (\$200 to \$509 million per year). These are upper bounds, not costs: setbacks limit drain tile, not farming, and production costs are not yet subtracted.

\item {\textbf{\color{ndsu} Most wetland basins are small.}} About three in four mapped wetland basins on easement tracts are under one acre, but together they make up only about 14\% of wetland acres. This matters for a potential FWS consolidation pilot, which may target basins under one acre.

\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has bought perpetual wetland easements in the Prairie Pothole Region with Federal Duck Stamp funds under the Small Wetlands Acquisition Program since 1958. Under this program landowners receive a one-time payment and agree that protected wetlands cannot be drained, filled, leveled, or burned (\href{https://www.federalregister.gov/citation/91-FR-46795}{91 FR 46795}).

FWS records each easement as a realty tract: the parcel of land covered by the agreement with the landowner. The eased wetlands are only part of each tract. FWS mapping data give the tract boundary and the total eased wetland acres inside it, but not where those wetlands are within the tract. % TODO: confirm tract definition against FWS realty documentation.

The National Wetland Inventory (NWI) dataset shows 4.2 million acres of wetlands in North Dakota (\autoref{fig:nwi_nd}). This total includes lakes and other open water, as well as wetlands that are not under easement currently.

\section{Drain Tile Setbacks}

Drain tile setbacks were not written into most original easement agreements. In most cases these drain tile setback distances were not provided to the landowner at the time the easement was purchased. FWS later set them through internal guidance and a 2020 Director's memo, and on May 13, 2024, the Service issued a final rule that codified the process by which landowners could request, and the Service would provide, drain tile setbacks under wetland easement contracts (\href{https://www.federalregister.gov/citation/91-FR-46795}{91 FR 46795}; \href{https://www.federalregister.gov/citation/89-FR-41336}{89 FR 41336}). The Service calculates the lateral effect with the van Schilfgaarde equation and applies it by landscape position. Upslope of the wetland, the setback is the calculated distance, but it stops at the wetland's catchment boundary if it reaches past it. Downslope, the full calculated distance always applies.

The calculation of the FWS tile setback distance is comparable to the Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) lateral effect distance, since both use the van Schilfgaarde equation. However, NRCS uses fixed, published inputs and a 500-ft cap under a ``minimal effect'' standard, where a 12-inch drawdown is allowed over a 14-day period (PPR210-650-H, 2022). FWS calculates each site individually under a ``must not be drained'' standard, cuts the setback off at the catchment boundary upslope, and applies the full distance downslope (FWS Director's Memo, 2020; 50 CFR 25.24). The NRCS assumptions are listed in \autoref{app:nrcs}.

We followed the formula specified in Title 210, National Engineering Handbook (PPR210-650-H, May 2022) and computed the lateral effect distances based on field and wetland soil types under the NRCS and FWS standards, which we approximate below.

The median setback distance is roughly 2.5 times larger under the FWS standard than under the NRCS standard (\autoref{tab:setback_county}). The only parameter changed when computing the setback distance under the FWS standard is the drawdown level, which we assumed to be 1.2 inches over a 14-day period. The setback distance needs to be calculated for each tract separately based on its field and wetland soil types; for simplicity, we used the dominant field and wetland soil types, meaning the soil types that cover the most acreage in each tract according to the USDA soil survey (SSURGO).\footnote{We assume a shallow tile line, 2 ft deep with a 3-inch diameter. Deeper, larger tile (7 ft, 12 inches) produces wider setbacks: a median of 290 ft under the FWS standard.} In practice, setbacks are computed from every soil type in a tract and would vary across the tract (NRCS National Engineering Handbook, Part 650, Chapter 19). Our estimates are a rough approximation of the most likely setback for each tract.

\input{tables/setback_county.tex}

Based on the calculated setback distances in \autoref{tab:setback_county}, we calculated the setback buffer around the eased wetlands, kept within each tract's boundary. This buffer represents the area where farmers cannot place tile. \autoref{fig:tract_buffer} shows an example for a single tract. In this 160-acre tract, NWI maps 22.7 acres of wetlands. A 180-ft setback adds 76.6 acres, so wetlands and setbacks together cover 99 acres, or 62\% of the tract.

\section{How Many Acres Are Affected?}

Figures of 4.2 and 4.5 million acres have been cited for the area governed by setbacks. Depending on what is counted, the number could be very different (\autoref{tab:acreage}). Easement tracts cover about 6.4 million acres, but only 1.03 million of those are eased wetlands. Adding our estimated FWS setback buffers around those wetlands gives about 4.0 million acres, which is close to the cited range. Using NRCS-standard setbacks instead gives about 2.3 million acres. The 4.2 million figure also matches total NWI wetland acres statewide, eased or not, so it is possible the cited number counts something else.

Our 2.3 to 4.0 million acre range should be treated with caution. Even the high end assumes FWS setbacks around every mapped wetland in easement tracts, and setbacks computed from full soil maps or for other tile designs would differ. Our totals also exclude about 2,050 FWS grassland easement tracts in North Dakota (about 0.66 million documented acres), which carry different restrictions.

Of the 4.0 million acres inside wetlands and setbacks, an average of 1.57 million acres (about 40\%) were planted to crops from 2021 to 2025, based on USDA's satellite-derived Cropland Data Layer. The rest is open water, wetland vegetation, grassland and pasture, fallow, and other land cover. Under NRCS setbacks, 0.62 million of the 2.3 million acres (about 27\%) were cropped.

\section{Potential Lost Returns}

To gauge what is at stake for producers, we valued the cropped land inside wetlands and setbacks in two ways. First, at county average cash rents and land values (\autoref{tab:cropland_rent_year}), this land rents for about \$102 million per year in total (\$65 per acre) and is worth about \$3.8 billion (\$2,400 per acre).

Second, we estimated gross crop revenue (\autoref{tab:revenue_crop_mean}). We matched the crops grown on this land each year, from the Cropland Data Layer, with USDA NASS surveyed county yields and state marketing year prices. Crops grown in and next to wetlands yield less than the rest of the field, so we reduced county yields by 15\%, based on yield-monitor data from prairie pothole fields in Saskatchewan (\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agsy.2024.104169}{Boldt et al., 2025}). Revenue averages \$509 million per year, or about \$330 per cropped acre. Soybeans, corn, spring wheat, and canola account for 87\% of the total. Both tables use FWS-standard setbacks. Under NRCS-standard setbacks, fewer acres are affected, and the totals fall to about \$41 million per year in rent, \$1.5 billion in land value, and \$200 million per year in revenue; per-acre values are nearly the same.

\input{tables/cropland_rent_summary_year.tex}

\input{tables/revenue_summary_crop_mean.tex}

These numbers are rough upper bounds and should not be read as the cost of the setbacks, for three reasons:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Setbacks limit tile, not farming.} Most of this land is still being cropped. The cost of a setback is the yield gain a producer gives up by not tiling, plus the extra cost of farming around wet areas, not the full value of the crop.
\item \textbf{Revenue is not profit.} Revenue is before production costs such as seed, fertilizer, fuel, and labor. Cash rent is a rough indicator of the net return to the land.
\item \textbf{County averages overstate wetland ground.} Wetland ground likely yields, rents, and sells for less than the county average, even after our 15\% discount.
\end{itemize}

These estimates may not reflect the performance of any particular field or farm.

\section{Next Steps}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Net returns.} Subtract production costs, using USDA costs of production and/or NDSU crop budgets, to estimate lost net returns rather than gross revenue.
\item \textbf{Cost of farming around wetlands.} Irregular field shapes increase overlap of seed, fertilizer, and spray, along with fuel and time. Boldt et al. (2025) estimate these overlap and nuisance costs from field path data. We could measure how irregular each field is, or simulate equipment paths with and without setbacks.
\item \textbf{Which wetlands still hold water.} NWI maps are often decades old and may not show current wetland extents. Satellite water records at 10 to 30 meter resolution could show how many mapped basins have held water in recent years.
\item \textbf{Range for the acreage estimate.} Narrow the 2.3 to 4.0 million acre range by computing setbacks from full soil maps and for other tile designs.
\item \textbf{Consolidation pilot.} Because setbacks are a fixed width around each basin, many small basins carry more setback acreage than one larger basin of the same total size. The net effect of consolidation is the land and field efficiency gained by removing small basins and their setbacks, minus the land taken by the new wetland and its setback. To estimate this, we need from FWS: which basins qualify (size, type, how often they hold water); how many acres of new wetland are required per acre removed; where the new wetland may be placed; what setback applies to it; who pays for construction and paperwork; and results from the pilot so far.
\end{itemize}

\section{NRCS Lateral Effect Assumptions}\label{app:nrcs}

NRCS runs the van Schilfgaarde equation with a fixed set of standard assumptions for the whole Prairie Pothole Region:

\begin{description}

\item[Drawdown:] The water table must fall 12 inches in 14 days, based on the wetland hydrology indicators in the USACE regional supplement. \textit{(U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. 2010. Regional Supplement to the Corps of Engineers Wetland Delineation Manual: Great Plains Region (Version 2.0). ERDC/EL TR-10-1. U.S. Army Engineer Research and Development Center, Vicksburg, MS.)}

\item[Impermeable layer:] Assumed at 10 ft, following national guidance and field data showing conductivity drops sharply between 9 and 14 ft in PPR glacial till. \textit{(USDA-NRCS. 2021. Hydrology Tools for Wetland Identification and Analysis. Title 210, National Engineering Handbook, Part 650, Chapter 19. Washington, DC; van der Kamp, G., and M. Hayashi. 2009. Groundwater-wetland ecosystem interaction in the semiarid glaciated plains of North America. Hydrogeology Journal 17:203--214.)}

\item[Deep conductivity:] Extended to 10 ft without modification. \textit{(USDA-NRCS. 2022. Prairie Pothole Region Supplement to Title 210, National Engineering Handbook, Part 650, Chapter 19 (PPR210-650-H), PPR650.1909I(1)(v).)}

\item[Surface storage:] 0.12 inches for mineral flats, organic flats, and recharge depressions, and 0 for discharge and slope wetlands, which are fed by groundwater rather than ponded runoff. \textit{(Gilley, J.E. 2018. Surface Detention on Cropland, Rangeland, and Conservation Reserve Program Areas. Biological Systems Engineering: Papers and Publications 562. University of Nebraska--Lincoln. \url{http://digitalcommons.unl.edu/biosysengfacpub/562}; Richardson, J.L., and M.J. Vepraskas. 2001. Wetland Soils: Genesis, Hydrology, Landscapes, and Classification. Lewis Publishers, Boca Raton, FL; Pali, A.K. 2013. Evaluation of Non-Steady Subsurface Drainage Equations for Heterogeneous Saline Soils: A Case Study. IOSR Journal of Agriculture and Veterinary Science 6(5):45--52.)}

\item[Soil properties:] Taken from NASIS through the Rosetta pedotransfer functions for mineral soils, and from regional averages by texture (peat, mucky peat, muck) for organic soils. Organic soil water retention parameters come from Liu and Lennartz, and drainable porosity from the van Genuchten equation. \textit{(Schaap, M.G., F.J. Leij, and M.T. van Genuchten. 2001. Rosetta: a computer program for estimating soil hydraulic parameters with hierarchical pedotransfer functions. Journal of Hydrology 251:163--176. \url{https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-1694(01)00466-8}; Liu, H., and B. Lennartz. 2019. Hydraulic properties of peat soils along a bulk density gradient: a meta study. Hydrological Processes 33:101--114. \url{https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.13314}; van Genuchten, M.Th. 1980. A closed-form equation for predicting the hydraulic conductivity of unsaturated soils. Soil Science Society of America Journal 44:892--898; Verry, E.S., D.H. Boelter, J. P\"aiv\"anen, D.S. Nichols, T. Malterer, and A. Gafni. 2011. Physical properties of organic soils. In Kolka, R.K., et al. (eds.), Peatland Biogeochemistry and Watershed Hydrology at the Marcell Experimental Forest. CRC Press, Boca Raton, FL, pp. 135--176.)}

\item[Map unit rating:] Map units are rated on their highest-value component that makes up 10\% or more of the unit. \textit{(USDA-NRCS. 2022. PPR210-650-H, PPR650.1909I(1)(viii).)}

\item[Rounding and cap:] Lateral effect distances are rounded up to the next 10 ft and capped at 500 ft, based on HGM buffer distances. \textit{(USDA-NRCS. 1999. Interim Functional Assessment Model for Lake Dakota Sand Plains, Version 2.2. Prairie Pothole Wetland Team, Jamestown, ND; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. 2011. Regional Guidebook for Applying the Hydrogeomorphic Approach to Assessing the Functions of Headwater Slope Wetlands on the South Carolina Coastal Plain. ERDC/EL TR-11-11; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. 2013. Regional Guidebook for Applying the Hydrogeomorphic Approach to Assessing the Functions of Flat and Seasonally Inundated Depression Wetlands on the Highland Rim. ERDC/EL TR-13-12.)}

\end{description}

Because these inputs are fixed, users change only the drain depth, drain diameter, and soil map units in the NRCS Lateral Effect Database calculator.
